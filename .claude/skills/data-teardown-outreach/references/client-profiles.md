# Client Profiles — who to target, in detail

This is the detailed companion to Step 1 of the skill. Use it to build target lists and
to judge whether a specific prospect is worth a teardown. The goal is always the same:
**a business with data it can't analyze, budget to pay, an owner who reads their own
inbox, a recurring reporting need, and public data you can use for a hook.**

A note on economics that applies to every vertical: the operator has a low cost base, so
a price that feels *cheap* to a US/EU client is strong income for him. That makes almost
any paying US/EU business viable — but it does **not** rescue prospects that have no
budget *at all*. Arbitrage lowers the price barrier; it doesn't create budget where there
is none. So always target businesses where money actually exists, and sell the
*specialization*, letting the price be a pleasant surprise rather than the pitch.

## 1. E-commerce — the primary, largest slice

**Who:** Shopify, Amazon (FBA), WooCommerce, and other online sellers; DTC brands.

**The sweet spot:** a real business, roughly a few thousand dollars a month up to a few
hundred thousand a month in revenue, **owner-operated or a tiny team**. Big enough to
have data and money; small enough that the founder reads their own email. *Not* the
mega-brands (they have analysts/agencies) and *not* the near-zero hobby shops.

**What they do:** sell physical or digital products online.

**Their pain:** drowning in Shopify/Stripe/ads/inventory data with no analyst; decisions
made on gut. They already pay for apps (Triple Whale, Helium 10, etc.), so paying for
insight is a familiar idea.

**Public-data hook:** Shopify exposes the full catalog at `/<domain>/products.json`
(no auth). You can analyze assortment structure, price ladder/gaps, sold-out-but-listed
items, launch cadence, and tag/type concentration — plus reviews for complaint themes.
Amazon and others are harder but reviews and pricing are still visible.

**Money:** strong and fast. Individuals pay $50–200/mo readily; per-report works too.

**How to find them:** niche DTC communities, "roast my store" threads, Shopify app
reviews, product-hunt-style launches, Instagram/TikTok shop links, or scraping category
lists. **Farm a tight sub-niche** (e.g., Shopify skincare $50k–500k/mo, candles, coffee,
pet, jewelry, supplements) so the teardown speaks their language.

## 2. Marketing agencies & freelance marketers — the best money

**Who:** small marketing/ad agencies, freelance performance marketers, fractional CMOs.

**What they do:** run or advise on marketing (ads, email, SEO) for client brands.

**Their pain:** they rebuild the *same* client report every week — a genuine, repeated,
deadline-driven grind. They bill clients, so a reporting tool is a cost of doing business,
not a discretionary luxury.

**Public-data hook:** their own site/case studies; or skip the teardown and pitch a
**white-label** arrangement — you produce the reports, they resell under their brand.

**Money:** excellent. $50–200+/mo or per-report, and a single white-label agency deal can
deliver several end clients at once (high leverage for a solo operator). Reachable online
and terminally present in marketing communities.

**Why they're often the best first target:** repetitive reporting + real budget + they
decide alone + easy to reach. When in doubt between agencies and direct e-commerce
brands, agencies usually win on money and speed.

## 3. Nonprofits — secondary, but the richest public data (and a mission pull)

**Who:** aim at **mid-to-large nonprofits ($1M+ budgets)** and foundations, and —
often the best sub-target — the **grant writers and nonprofit consultants** who serve
many orgs and bill for it.

**What they do:** run donor/grant-funded programs and must report impact to donors,
boards, and grant-makers.

**Their pain:** grant reporting, impact reports, and board decks are recurring,
deadline-driven, high-stakes, and they usually have no data team. Grant/impact reporting
is the wedge — it's the thing they *must* produce because funding depends on it.

**Public-data hook (the best of any vertical):** US nonprofits file **IRS Form 990s that
are fully public** — multi-year revenue, expenses, program-expense ratio, fundraising
cost per dollar raised, and executive comp. Pull them from **ProPublica's Nonprofit
Explorer** or **Candid/GuideStar**. You can produce a legitimate financial teardown
("program-expense ratio slipped three years running; fundraising cost per dollar rose")
with zero trust required and nothing asked of them first.

**Money:** weaker and slower than agencies/e-commerce for *small* orgs (budget competes
with programs; buying is committee-driven). The arbitrage helps, but still target orgs
and consultants with real budgets. **Avoid tiny grant-only orgs** where overhead spend is
a board-optics problem.

**Note:** the same solo-cost-base arbitrage that makes US nonprofits viable makes agencies
and e-commerce *even more* lucrative — so pick nonprofits for the public-data hook or the
mission, not because the money is better.

## 4. Other data-rich, analyst-poor businesses — opportunistic

The universal pattern applies far beyond the three above. Any of these can qualify one at
a time; treat them as opportunistic sends, not a core niche, unless one starts converting.

- **Small SaaS founders** — churn, MRR, cohorts, activation. Real money, but they're
  technical and may DIY, so the insight must beat "ask the LLM to write Python."
- **Bookkeepers / fractional CFOs** — repetitive financial reporting for their clients;
  community-rich; conservative about AI touching numbers (longer trust climb).
- **Local multi-location businesses** (gyms, clinics, dental/vet groups, franchises) —
  POS/appointment data, little analysis. Reachable, budget varies.
- **Real estate teams & investors** — property, comps, and transaction data.
- **Course creators / membership & subscription businesses** — retention and engagement
  data they rarely mine.
- **E-commerce aggregators, 3PLs, property managers, event/ticketing operators** — all
  sit on transaction data with no analyst.

**General heuristic:** anyone who would email you a spreadsheet and say "I wish I
understood this better" is a candidate. Score them with the checklist below before
investing in a teardown.

---

## 5. Anti-targets — skip these

- **Whales** — large enough to have an internal data/BI team or an agency. They won't
  answer cold email. Use them as *examples* to practice teardowns, never as targets.
- **Hobbyists / micro-operators** — no discretionary budget at any price.
- **Broke or grant-only nonprofits** — overhead spending is politically hard; no budget.
- **Purely offline / no-data businesses** — nothing to analyze.
- **Sensitive-data-only targets** (health PII, regulated finance) — too much compliance
  overhead to start with; revisit once established.

## 6. The qualification checklist

Score each prospect before spending time. Aim for **at least 3 clear "yes"** — ideally 5.

1. **Data?** Do they have enough data (scale/revenue) that analysis is worth doing?
2. **Money?** Is there real budget — a business with revenue, or a funded org, that buys
   tools?
3. **Reachable?** Will the actual decision-maker see the email (owner/small team, real
   name findable)?
4. **Recurring?** Does the reporting need repeat, so a report can become a retainer?
5. **Hook?** Can you produce a genuine teardown from their public data before contact?

Fewer than 3 → skip and move on. A smaller list of well-qualified prospects with real
names beats a large generic blast every time.
