# 03 — Credibility & guardrails

Your only durable asset is trust. One wrong claim in a report ends a client and poisons the
referrals that travel inside a niche. These are the non-negotiables — they mirror the
outreach skill's guardrails and your engine's own architecture.

## The guardrails

### 1. Numbers come from code, never from the model (or your gut)
Every figure in a deliverable must trace to a deterministic computation. The LLM (and you)
*phrase* findings; neither eyeballs data and states a number, because both will occasionally
be confidently wrong. This mirrors the product: deterministic math, generated prose. If you
can't point to the computation behind a number, don't ship the number.

### 2. Public / observational findings are hypotheses
Anything from public data or non-randomised data is "worth checking against your numbers,"
never a stated fact about their business. Over-claiming on public data is the fastest way to
lose a technical reader. (Same rule as the teardown outreach.)

### 3. Correlation is not causation
Say "associated with" / "driver of," not "causes," unless you ran the causal workflow with a
refutation check (data-science note 10) — and even then, state the assumptions. This is the
single most common way analysts lose credibility with a sharp client.

### 4. Match claims to sample size
Don't ship a churn model trained on 12 cancellations or an RFM on 80 customers as if it were
robust. State n. When the data can't support a method, say so and offer the descriptive
alternative. Honesty about limits reads as *more* expert, not less.

### 5. Always include a genuine positive
An all-criticism analysis reads as manufactured. Real analysis finds what works too.

### 6. Protect personal data
Never collect or retain customer/donor PII. Work on behaviour and money. Tell clients to
strip PII from exports before sending — a trust signal and a liability shield.

### 7. Never manufacture a finding
Don't cherry-pick a split date, a segment, or a time window to produce a "result." If the
data says "nothing significant changed," that *is* the finding. Fabricated significance is
the big-data trap (data-science note 02) weaponised — and it's malpractice.

## The credibility flywheel
Correct, honest findings → client trusts you → referral inside the niche → warmer next lead.
Your knowledge-sharing (blog/socials → your site) compounds the same trust at a distance.
Every guardrail above protects this flywheel; every shortcut breaks it.

## The one-line test before you send
> *"If their in-house analyst pulled this apart line by line, would every number survive,
> and is every claim exactly as strong as the data supports — no stronger?"*
If yes, ship. If not, weaken the claim until it's true, or go compute more.
